# Dickinson & Whitman

**Emily Dickinson**, "I'm Nobody! Who are you?" (c. 1861)

> I'm Nobody! Who are you?
> Are you – Nobody – too?

**Walt Whitman**, "Song of Myself" (1855)

> I celebrate myself, and sing myself,
> And what I assume you shall assume,

## The Contrast

Both poets speak straight to "you," but they want opposite things from the reader. Dickinson speaks quietly. She shrinks the self to "Nobody" and pulls the reader into a private pair: two hidden people keeping a secret from the public "Frog." Her lines are short and use hymn meter, and her dashes make each pause feel deliberate.

Whitman speaks loudly. He enlarges the self until it holds the reader too: "every atom belonging to me as good belongs to you." His long, unrhymed lines sprawl like the democratic crowd he celebrates.

Dickinson compresses and Whitman expands. She finds the infinite by withdrawing, and he finds it by embracing. Between them they mark the two poles of American poetry: the closed room and the open road.
